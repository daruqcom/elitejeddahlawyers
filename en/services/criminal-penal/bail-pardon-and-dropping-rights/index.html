Bail, pardon, and dropping rights in Jeddah: three fundamentally different tracks

Three distinct tracks can end or substantially reduce a criminal case: temporary release on bail during investigation or trial, waiver of the private right by the victim themselves, and pardon by a guardian, the competent authority, or the victim's family in qisas cases. Each track has its own conditions and an entirely different effect on the case's outcome, and clients frequently confuse them, while precisely distinguishing between them is what sets the correct strategy from day one.

Release on bail: the procedural details

In many non-major cases, temporary release can be requested against a financial guarantee or a guarantor (personal or financial), in place of continued detention. The application for temporary release is submitted to the investigating prosecutor or the head of the department, setting out why the grounds for detention no longer apply, such as a known residence or a sufficient financial guarantee, and the implementing regulation requires urgent applications to be answered within 24 hours. Whether bail is granted depends on the competent authority's assessment of the charge's severity and flight risk, and delaying the application can lead to extended detention before the case is presented to court, which is why acting quickly after arrest is decisive for a bail application's success.

Waiving the private right

In offenses that carry a private right for the victim, such as qadhf or certain minor injury or bounced-cheque offenses, the victim may waive their private right, which can close the file entirely or substantially reduce the sentence, even though the state's public right may still proceed in some cases and courts vary in how they weigh its effect on the public right. What matters practically is drafting the waiver in a legally sound way that protects both sides' interests and submitting it to the correct authority at the correct time, since an undocumented waiver or one submitted to the wrong authority may not be given effect.

Pardon in qisas cases

In intentional homicide cases, the victim's next of kin hold the right to fully pardon the offender from qisas, either for free or in exchange for diyah, a pivotal decision that shifts the entire course of the case from execution to an agreed or judicially assessed diyah, alongside a separate discretionary penalty for the public right. Managing pardon negotiations between the two families requires great sensitivity, combining legal and human dimensions at once, and we take on this role in a way that preserves both sides' dignity and statutory rights together.

Royal and princely pardons

In certain offenses, and on specific occasions such as national and religious holidays, a royal or princely pardon may be issued covering defined categories of convicts under conditions set by the decision itself (such as the nature of the offense, remaining sentence, and the convict's conduct during execution). Tracking these occasions and submitting applications to benefit from them in time requires close monitoring of issued decisions and filing through the correct official channels.

Which track fits your situation?

Choosing the right track depends on the type of offense (does it carry a private right at all?), the stage of the case (are you still detained, or has a judgment already been issued?), and whether the injured party is willing to settle. Sometimes combining two tracks is possible, such as requesting bail while negotiating a waiver of the private right, and this integrated assessment is what we provide in the first consultation.

The guarantor: who qualifies and who doesn't

A personal guarantor undertakes only to produce the accused when required, while a financial guarantor secures a fixed sum that is forfeited if the accused fails to appear. The competent authority typically requires the guarantor to have a known place of residence and sufficient financial standing to cover the guaranteed amount if a financial guarantee is requested, and may reject a guarantor with a prior record or one who is themselves under suspicion in similar cases. Selecting the right guarantor and preparing their documents in advance considerably speeds up the decision on a release application.

When bail applications are typically refused

The competent authority usually refuses bail in major offenses posing a danger to society, or where there is a serious indicator of flight risk or interference with the investigation (such as threatening witnesses or destroying evidence), or where the accused has a similar prior record. In these cases, the lawyer's work centers on rebutting these concerns with concrete evidence, such as a fixed residence, stable employment, and no prior record, rather than simply repeating the bail request without new grounds.
